# Speaking brief — Black Mountain / Parker County
### For answering questions out loud. Prepared September 3, 2026.

Each entry: what you'd be asked, what to say, the number and where it comes from, and the
limit — the thing you must not claim. The limits are the point. Every number here has one,
and knowing it is what lets you answer a hostile question without retreating.

---

## If you remember nothing else

**Three things are always true and always safe to say:**

1. **Nobody has filed a number.** No capacity is on record for the Parker site, no permit
   application is on file with the county, no ERCOT queue position has been announced. That is a
   fact about the record, not a guess.
2. **Parker County has refused abatements** — Resolution 26-25. The county already did the one
   thing fully within its power.
3. **The water question is live and the abatement question is closed.** If you have thirty seconds,
   that sentence is the briefing.

**And one habit:** when you don't know, say "I don't know, and here's what would settle it." That
sentence has carried this project further than any figure in it.

## 1. "You published $364 million. Now you don't. Which is it?"

**Say this.** The $364 million was a scenario multiplied by an assumption — a gigawatt campus
extrapolated from acreage, times an 80% tax abatement. Neither was ever filed with anyone. The
county then refused abatements outright, so the assumption stopped describing reality. We didn't
delete the number; it's still in the grid, in the cell where "1 GW, acreage-implied" meets "80%
Chapter 312," and the June briefing is still online, unchanged, so anyone can read what we said.

**The source.** Resolution 26-25; county judge on the record, June 30, 2026: *"there will be zero
tax abatements for data centers in Parker County."*

**The limit.** Don't oversell the correction as proof of rigor — we published the number for three
months and residents were right to flag it. Say that plainly if asked. It costs nothing and it is
the reason to believe the rest.

**If pressed.** "What changed isn't the arithmetic — the model is the same. What changed is that
one input became a known fact instead of an assumption."

## 2. "How big is this thing?"

**Say this.** Nobody has said, publicly, on paper. The TCEQ registration authorizes five gas
turbines. The 75-megawatt figure everyone repeats — including us, in June — comes from testimony in
open court on May 26, not from the permit.

**The source.** TCEQ Air New Source Registration 179422 (RN112172408). The Central Registry entry
shows status, holder and address — **no capacity**. The Central File Room returns no documents.

**The limit.** **Do not say the permit authorizes 75 MW.** It's the single most likely place to be
caught. And don't treat 75 MW as a ceiling either: the same operator's Bowie County plant, filed
under the same standard permit, discloses roughly **446 MW**, and three of its Tarrant registrations
are named for Siemens SGT-800 machines rated 45–62 MW each. Five of those would be 250 MW or more.

**If pressed.** "The number exists in a file at TCEQ. We've asked for it. Until it's public, we
label it as testimony, because that's what it is."

## 3. "How many acres?"

**Say this.** About 2,081 acres assembled. The 18 parcels that got corrected notices when the
appraisal district stripped the agricultural exemption on May 15 total 2,075.28.

**The source.** The May 2026 corrected notices for the 18; Parker CAD's certified roll for the
19th — parcel R000127494, 6.53 acres, bought from the same seller, sequential in the same block,
geocoded identically to its neighbour, and never issued a notice.

**The limit.** We have no deed for that 19th parcel. Same seller, adjacent, sequential is strong but
circumstantial. If someone runs title and it isn't part of the transaction, the number is 2,075.28.
Say so before they do.

**Also worth knowing.** Two CAD documents disagree by 0.39 acres on one parcel — the May 2026 notice
reads 155.763 where the 2025 roll reads 155.373. Transposed digits. We use the newer document.

## 4. "How much water will it use?"

**Say this.** The land can legally yield between about 52 and 520 million gallons a year — roughly
0.14 to 1.4 million gallons a day — and the exact figure depends on how thick the Trinity is under
that tract. What matters more is that closed-loop cooling doesn't solve it. It moves the water to
wherever the electricity is made.

**The numbers.** At 75 MW of computing load: an evaporative hall consumes about **197 million
gallons a year** on site. A closed-loop hall consumes about **8 million** on site — but **205
million** once you count the water consumed generating its power on a combined cycle or grid
supply. Essentially the same total, relocated.

**The source.** UTGCD Rule 5.2 (500 gallons per contiguous controlled acre × aquifer thickness,
floor 25,000, cap 250,000). Generation water from TWDB's *Power Generation Water Use in Texas*:
0.05 gal/kWh for a gas turbine with wet NOx control, negligible with dry control, 0.23 for combined
cycle with a cooling tower. Cross-checked against NREL/TP-6A20-50900.

**The limit.** We don't have the aquifer thickness at the tract yet, so the ceiling is a range, not a
number. **And do not repeat the "five million gallons a day" figure** heard in public comment — it
is unsourced and we don't use it.

**The local sting, if you want one line that lands.** "They propose to make their own power on that
tract. If they do, the water behind the power comes out of the same aquifer — which means whether
those turbines use wet or dry NOx control is a groundwater decision buried inside an air permit that
was issued in two and a half weeks with no public process."

## 5. "What will it cost the county?"

**Say this.** Under the county's own resolution there's no abatement, so the honest question isn't
what we lose — it's what we collect and whether it covers what the project costs us in services.

**The numbers, unabated.** Phase 1 (~$750M of improvements): about **$3.8 million a year** to the
county, hospital district, junior college, ESD-1 and lateral road — $38 million over ten years. Plus
about **$6.6 million a year to Weatherford ISD**, which no county abatement could touch anyway. A
Fort Worth-class campus at $10 billion would be roughly $506 million and $879 million over ten years
respectively.

**The source.** 2025 adopted rates; improvements taxed at 85% of value; model at
`profile_2026_09/RCO_05g_scenarios.R`.

**The limit — important.** **We have not quantified the service costs.** Emergency response for an
industrial site, road wear, water-system load are all real, and we have not put a dollar figure on
any of them. Do not assert that the project costs more than it pays. We don't know that yet.

**If someone says "so it's good for us."** "On the revenue side, probably yes, and the caps don't
even claw it back — new construction is excluded from the 3.5% calculation in its first year. That's
why the question is net, and the net is what nobody has computed."

## 6. "Why can't the county just stop it?"

**Say this.** Texas counties have no zoning. The counties that tried land-use prohibitions got sued
and reversed.

**The record.** Hill County passed a one-year moratorium in May 2026 and rescinded it within weeks
after a developer sued for more than $100 million. Tom Green abandoned its plans after watching.
Hood and Hays considered moratoriums in February and never adopted them; Hood has denied plats
instead and is in litigation. What holds up is cities using zoning — El Paso's 300-foot residential
separation, Forney's 1,000-foot buffer, Lewisville's special-use permits.

**The mechanism, if someone wants the why.** HB 2127 (2023) created Civil Practice & Remedies Code
Chapter 102A: any person — or a trade association on a member's behalf — who suffers an injury
"actual or threatened" from a county ordinance can sue, governmental immunity is waived, and the
claimant recovers attorney's fees on winning while the county recovers only if the suit was
frivolous.

**The limit.** SB 2858, which would have added attorney-general enforcement and let the comptroller
withhold a county's sales-tax funds, **died in House Calendars**. Don't describe that as current law.

## 7. "What has the court actually done?"

**Say this.** Two things, both real. Resolution 26-25 refuses tax abatements for data centers. And
the June 9 resolution opposes open-loop evaporative cooling and high-volume potable water use.

**The limit — say this part too.** The resolution binds no future court, and it does not bind the
hospital district, the junior college, ESD-1 or the lateral road fund. Each of those grants its own
Chapter 312 agreement. That's why asking them to match it is on our list.

## 9. "What's the state doing about any of this?"

**Say this.** On August 3 the Governor ordered PUC and ERCOT to audit every data center in the
interconnection queue before any more move forward. ERCOT is working to finish by December 10.

**The scale.** Roughly 250–300 projects under audit, against about 474 gigawatts of interconnection
requests — more than five times ERCOT's record peak demand — of which about 90% is data centers.
Each project must file its incentives, power draw, water use, community mitigation and ownership.

**On the tax breaks.** Tax Code §151.359 and §151.3595 exempt qualifying data centers from sales tax
on equipment, cooling and **the electricity itself**. The Governor cannot repeal them — they're
statutes, administered by the Comptroller, who is separately elected. He's named repeal a 2027
legislative priority. What can claw money back is an audit: **138 facilities certified, 20 audited,
6 of those 20 out of compliance.**

**The limit.** The August 3 directive is a pause on advancement through interconnection, not a
moratorium. Don't call it a ban.

## 10. "What's a 391 commission and will it help?"

**Say this.** It's a planning commission two or more counties can form by resolution. It can study,
recommend, review state and federal grant applications, and require state agencies to coordinate
with it. It cannot tax, cannot zone, and adds no authority the counties don't already have.

**The honest verdict** — and it was said in open court on July 13: a 391 is *"perception of doing
something without actually accomplishing the main goal."* What it does is add hurdles and delay, and
create a table the state has to sit at.

**Where it stands.** Two routes: a new Parker–Hood sub-regional commission, or working inside the
North Central Texas COG, which is itself a 391. As of mid-July the county had no local partner
secured. Our record stops there.

## 11. "Are you against data centers?"

**Say this.** No — against a process that put a 2,000-acre industrial assembly and a power plant
next to people who had no way to know it was coming. If the water, the power and the money are
disclosed and the community has a seat, that's a different conversation.

**Don't** get drawn into defending a position the briefing doesn't take. It's a document about
process and evidence.

## 13. The ask, compressed

If someone asks what you want the court to do, there are seven, and the first four are letters or
filings the county attorney would prepare:

1. Ask TCEQ for the registration file — and whether it was evaluated against the 25-tons-per-year
   NOx threshold that applies in the DFW **Severe** ozone nonattainment area, given testimony that
   the plant will run continuously.
2. Amend the water resolution from "no open-loop cooling" to a **total-system water** standard.
3. Put the county on the record with the groundwater district: notice of high-volume applications,
   contested-case intent, whole-system water disclosure.
4. File with PUC and ERCOT before the audit closes — publish results county by county, report water
   whole.
5. Settle the 391 route with a date certain.
6. Ask the hospital district, junior college, ESD-1 and lateral road fund to match Resolution 26-25;
   adopt a road-use and financial-assurance policy before construction traffic.
7. Adopt decommissioning terms — baseline survey before ground disturbance, removal to four feet, no
   salvage credit, security posted per phase, covenant running with the land.

**Correspondence to any agency outside county government is the county attorney's to draft and
send.** We supply example language and citations. We don't supply final drafts.
